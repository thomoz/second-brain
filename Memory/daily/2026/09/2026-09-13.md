**[Brain]** Likely downward pressure on the US dollar, but not automatically a crash.

If foreign central banks keep selling Treasuries, they usually receive US dollars and then may convert those dollars into euros, yen, gold, or other assets. That creates selling pressure on USD.

My read: steady foreign selling is bearish for long-term dollar dominance, especially if paired with US deficits, inflation risk, sanctions risk, or loss of confidence. But in a crisis, the dollar can still rise because it remains the main reserve and safe-haven currency.

Key point: falling foreign Treasury ownership weakens the dollar’s structural support, but the dollar only falls hard if private/global investors also stop wanting USD assets.

**[WhatsApp]** 61410868612@c.us: If Central banks are selling their treasuries because they're losing trust in the safety of those treasuries would it Stand to reason that private investors would feel the same way?
**[Brain]** Yes, but only partly.

If central banks are selling because they genuinely think Treasuries are no longer safe, private investors would probably notice and demand higher yields too.

But central-bank selling often has other causes: defending their currency, needing dollar liquidity, rebalancing reserves, reducing duration risk, or diversifying into gold. That does not automatically mean “Treasuries are unsafe.”

Private investors also judge differently. They care about yield, liquidity, credit risk, inflation risk, currency risk, and alternatives. Treasuries can still look attractive even if some central banks reduce exposure.

The key signal is not just “central banks are selling.” It’s whether buyers demand a lasting higher term premium, auctions weaken, the dollar falls, and gold/other reserve assets keep gaining.

**[WhatsApp]** 61410868612@c.us: The Central bank to selling treasuries at the moment because they don't want to run the risk of those treasuries being confiscated in the same way that they were in Russia would private investors then feel the same
**[Brain]** Yes, some would, but usually much less than central banks.

Central banks hold Treasuries as sovereign reserves, so confiscation/freezing risk is political and existential for them. Russia made that risk very visible.

Private investors care more about liquidity, yield, currency stability, legal protections, and whether they personally could be sanctioned. A normal US, Australian, European, Japanese fund manager is unlikely to fear Treasury confiscation.

But private investors in countries that might one day clash with the US, or ultra-wealthy people exposed to sanctions risk, may diversify more into gold, non-US assets, or assets held outside Western custody.

So the effect is strongest for governments and sanctioned-risk actors. For ordinary private capital, Treasuries probably remain attractive unless the US rule-of-law premium itself starts to look damaged.

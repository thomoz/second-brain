## Prompt for Market Wizards research:

## GLOBAL SMALL-CAP VALUE + GROWTH / MARKET WIZARDS RESEARCH

Find a new group of attractive publicly listed small-cap companies globally. I am happy to invest outside the US and want you to search broadly across Australia, UK, Europe, Singapore, Hong Kong, Canada, South Africa, Japan and other credible markets.

### PRIMARY SCREEN

Look for companies that preferably meet all of these conditions:

* Market capitalisation below US$1 billion.
* Profitable.
* Normalised P/E below 10.
* Evidence of future earnings or free-cash-flow growth.
* Sound balance sheet.
* Reasonable liquidity for a small-cap investor.
* An identifiable catalyst or reason earnings/value can improve.
* A plausible reason why the market may currently be undervaluing the company.
* Avoid companies already contained in my exclusion/master research list.

Trailing P/E is preferred. If a company only qualifies based on forward earnings, explicitly flag this and investigate how reliable those forecasts are.

Do not allow a low headline P/E alone to qualify a company.

Use current company filings, presentations, announcements, credible market data and other current sources. State the date of the financial information and share price being used.

---

# MARKET WIZARDS FRAMEWORK

Use the principles from the uploaded Market Wizards material throughout the research.

The three highest-priority principles are:

1. **Risk Management**
2. **Asymmetric Bets**
3. **Have an Edge**

Also incorporate:

* Variant perception: what might we understand differently from the investor selling the shares?
* Patience/selectivity: only pursue setups where several factors line up.
* Catalysts: cheap assets can remain cheap indefinitely without something changing.
* Technical/market confirmation where useful.
* Define what makes the thesis wrong before determining position size.

The objective is not merely to find cheap shares. It is to identify situations where:

**The downside is understandable and controllable, while the upside could be multiples of the downside if our thesis is correct.**

# IMPORTANT: CHECK THE 5-YEAR STOCK CHART EARLY

For every company that survives the initial quantitative screen, look at its **five-year stock-price chart near the beginning of the research process**, not at the end.

Report approximately:

* Current price.
* Five-year starting price.
* Five-year return.
* Major highs and lows.
* Approximate maximum drawdown where useful.
* Whether the stock has been trending up, sideways or down.
* Whether recent price strength represents a recovery from a long decline.

Do **not** automatically reject a company because the chart is bad.

Instead, treat a bad chart as a diagnostic warning.

If the five-year chart is poor, immediately investigate **why** before developing a bullish thesis.

Specifically investigate whether the decline was caused by:

* Falling EPS.
* Peak-cycle earnings reverting.
* Margin compression.
* Revenue growth without earnings growth.
* Poor acquisitions.
* Excessive acquisition spending.
* Equity dilution.
* Rising debt.
* Inventory problems.
* Working-capital problems.
* Higher interest expense.
* Weak cash conversion.
* Consumer or economic cyclicality.
* Commodity-price declines.
* Competitive deterioration.
* Loss of market share.
* Governance problems.
* Regulatory issues.
* Management mistakes.
* Structural deterioration in the industry.
* Temporary macroeconomic conditions.
* A simple valuation de-rating.

Compare the share-price history with **revenue, EPS, margins and free cash flow over the same five-year period**.

A particularly important warning sign is:

**Revenue rising substantially while EPS or free cash flow stagnates or declines.**

If that has happened, determine exactly why.

The chart should act as a **challenge to the fundamental thesis**, not merely an illustration.

If a company appears fundamentally excellent but its five-year chart is terrible, assume there may be something important we have not yet understood and investigate further.

# NORMALISE THE EARNINGS

Never rely blindly on the reported P/E.

Recalculate or estimate a **normalised earnings multiple**.

Strip out or separately identify:

* Asset-sale gains.
* Impairment reversals.
* Fair-value gains.
* One-off tax benefits.
* Insurance proceeds.
* Restructuring credits.
* Acquisition accounting gains.
* Exceptional foreign-exchange gains.
* Unusually favourable commodity prices.
* Other non-recurring items.

If reported EPS materially overstates recurring earnings, use adjusted recurring earnings instead.

Show:

**Headline P/E
Normalised P/E
Forward P/E**

where available.

If the headline P/E is below 10 but normalised P/E is above 10, the company **fails the original screen** unless there is a particularly compelling reason to keep it on a watchlist.

---

# CHECK WHETHER EARNINGS ARE AT A CYCLICAL PEAK

For commodity companies, shipping, automotive, construction, finance and other cyclical industries, do not automatically treat current earnings as sustainable.

Ask:

* Are commodity prices unusually high?
* Are margins above historical norms?
* Is utilisation unusually high?
* Are industry inventories unusually low?
* Are competitors currently earning abnormal returns?
* Did COVID or another unusual event create temporary economics?
* Would earnings remain attractive under mid-cycle assumptions?

Calculate a **normalised/mid-cycle earnings estimate** wherever appropriate.

A 5× P/E at peak earnings can be more expensive than a 12× P/E at trough earnings.

---

# REVENUE GROWTH IS NOT ENOUGH

Examine at least five years, where available, of:

* Revenue.
* EBITDA.
* EBIT.
* NPAT.
* EPS.
* Gross margin.
* Operating margin.
* Free cash flow.

Determine whether the company is genuinely compounding **per-share economic value**.

Pay particular attention to situations where:

**Revenue rises but EPS does not.**

Explain why this happened.

Possible causes include:

* Lower margins.
* Acquisitions.
* Dilution.
* Excess operating expenses.
* Higher financing costs.
* Poor acquisition economics.
* Working-capital consumption.

---

# SEGMENT ECONOMICS

Do not rely only on consolidated numbers.

Where segment information exists, break down:

* Revenue by segment.
* Profit by segment.
* Segment margins.
* Growth rates.
* Capital requirements.

Identify:

**The real economic engine of the company.**

Determine whether a weak or low-margin segment is disguising a much better business inside the company.

Likewise determine whether a high-growth segment is actually contributing meaningful profits.

Look for situations similar to MTO where the market may classify a company according to its visible legacy business while a different segment has quietly become the dominant profit generator.

This can create a strong **variant perception**.

---

# ORGANIC GROWTH VS ACQUISITION GROWTH

Separate organic growth from acquired growth wherever possible.

Do not praise 20% revenue growth if most of it came from purchasing another company.

For acquisitive businesses examine:

* Purchase price.
* Debt assumed.
* Shares issued.
* Goodwill created.
* Integration costs.
* Incremental EBIT/NPAT.
* Return on invested capital.
* Whether EPS per share actually increased.

Ask:

**Did shareholders become wealthier per share, or did the company simply become larger?**

---

# DILUTION

Examine the share count over at least five years.

Identify:

* Placement shares.
* Acquisition consideration shares.
* Rights issues.
* Employee options.
* Stock-based compensation.
* Convertible securities.
* Warrants.

Calculate approximate cumulative dilution.

A company growing profit 15% while increasing shares outstanding 12% is not producing 15% growth for existing shareholders.

Focus on **per-share growth**, not simply company growth.

---

# CASH FLOW QUALITY

Compare accounting earnings with cash.

Examine:

* Cash flow from operations.
* Free cash flow.
* Maintenance capex.
* Growth capex.
* Working-capital movements.
* Inventory.
* Receivables.
* Payables.

Investigate cases where CFO appears unusually strong because of:

* Inventory reductions.
* Payables increases.
* Receivables collection.
* Inventory financing.
* Bailment financing.
* Factoring.
* Other financing arrangements.

Create an approximate **owner earnings / recurring FCF** number where useful.

Do not assume NPAT equals distributable cash.

---

# BALANCE SHEET — INCLUDING HIDDEN LEVERAGE

Do not stop at reported “net debt”.

Look for:

* Bank debt.
* Lease liabilities.
* Inventory/bailment financing.
* Factoring.
* Supplier financing.
* Convertible debt.
* Pension liabilities.
* Deferred acquisition payments.
* Guarantees.
* Off-balance-sheet obligations.
* Project-level debt.

If management claims the company is “net cash,” check whether economically significant financing obligations sit outside that definition.

Assess whether the company could survive:

* A recession.
* 20–30% earnings decline.
* Higher interest rates.
* Commodity-price weakness.
* A temporary working-capital shock.

---

# CAPITAL ALLOCATION

Research management's record of deploying shareholder capital.

Look at:

* Acquisitions.
* Dividends.
* Buybacks.
* Equity issuance.
* Debt repayment.
* Capex.
* Asset sales.

Ask:

**Has management historically created value per share?**

Penalise companies that repeatedly issue shares, overpay for acquisitions or expand simply for the sake of becoming larger.

# COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE / EDGE

For every serious candidate, explain:

### What is the business advantage?

Examples:

* Lowest-cost producer.
* Distribution network.
* Switching costs.
* Niche expertise.
* Scarce licences.
* Network effects.
* Recurring revenue.
* Proprietary technology.
* Brand.
* Customer relationships.
* Geographic advantage.
* Structural industry consolidation.

Then answer the more important Market Wizards question:

### What is our variant perception?

State clearly:

**“The market appears to believe ________, whereas our research suggests ________.”**

If this sentence cannot be completed convincingly, we probably do not have an edge.

# GROWTH RUNWAY

Determine where future growth can realistically come from.

Possible sources include:

* Capacity expansion.
* Market-share gains.
* Geographic expansion.
* New products.
* Operating leverage.
* Margin normalisation.
* Cost reductions.
* New mines/assets/projects.
* Acquisitions.
* Industry recovery.
* Debt reduction.
* Higher asset utilisation.

Estimate how long the runway may last.

Do not simply extrapolate one unusually strong year.

Cheap companies can stay cheap.

Identify tangible catalysts such as:

* Earnings inflection.
* New capacity becoming operational.
* Debt reduction.
* Asset sale.
* Spin-off.
* Buyback.
* Dividend growth.
* New contract.
* Regulatory approval.
* New product.
* Acquisition integration.
* Margin recovery.
* Industry supply tightening.
* Restructuring.
* Management change.

Give an approximate timeframe where possible.

# GOVERNANCE AND MANAGEMENT

Investigate:

* Insider ownership.
* Management compensation.
* Related-party transactions.
* Auditor issues.
* Accounting restatements.
* Regulatory problems.
* Historical disclosure quality.
* Treatment of minority shareholders.
* Capital-allocation history.

Small caps deserve a higher governance hurdle than large caps.

# LIQUIDITY

Report approximately:

* Typical trading value/volume.
* Free float where available.
* Major shareholder concentration.

Flag companies where it may be difficult to build or exit a position without affecting the price.

Illiquidity is not automatically disqualifying, but the position should be smaller.

---

# THESIS INVALIDATION

Before suggesting an entry, explicitly state what observable events would prove the investment thesis wrong.

Examples:

* Key segment profit declines.
* Market share reverses.
* Debt rises above a defined threshold.
* Cash conversion disappears.
* Margins fall below a specified level.
* Acquisition economics disappoint.
* Project capex materially exceeds assumptions.
* Dilution accelerates.
* Competitive advantage weakens.
* Commodity economics fall below sustainable levels.

The invalidation point should come from the **business thesis**, not an arbitrary 10% or 20% stock-price stop.

---

# VALUATION AND ASYMMETRY

For the strongest candidates construct:

### Bear Case

Conservative earnings + depressed but reasonable multiple.

### Base Case

Normalised sustainable earnings + sensible historical/peer multiple.

### Bull Case

Successful catalyst/growth execution + reasonable higher multiple.

Show:

* Estimated EPS or FCF.
* Applied valuation multiple.
* Resulting valuation.
* Percentage downside/upside from current price.

Do not use unrealistic multiples merely to manufacture upside.

Seek situations where the plausible upside is materially greater than plausible fundamental downside.

---

# ENTRY PRICE

Do not automatically recommend buying immediately.

Distinguish between:

**Great company
Great investment
Great investment at this price**

They are not the same thing.

Estimate:

* Fair current value.
* Attractive entry range.
* Exceptional-value entry range.
* Price or fundamental confirmation that would justify paying more.

Where appropriate combine fundamental valuation with technical price structure.

---

# POSITION SIZING / RISK

Only after defining the fundamental invalidation point should position size be considered.

Use the general framework:

**Position size ≈ acceptable portfolio loss ÷ estimated loss if thesis is invalidated**

Prefer small initial positions when:

* Liquidity is poor.
* Earnings are cyclical.
* Catalysts are binary.
* Balance sheets are leveraged.
* The company operates in a higher-risk jurisdiction.

Do not allow enthusiasm about upside to override risk control.

---

# REJECTION DISCIPLINE

Explicitly reject or demote companies where research reveals:

* Normalised P/E is materially higher than headline P/E.
* Current earnings appear near cyclical peak.
* Revenue growth does not translate into per-share earnings.
* Cash conversion is poor.
* Balance-sheet risk is excessive.
* Persistent dilution.
* Weak governance.
* Acquisition-dependent growth without good returns.
* No clear edge.
* No identifiable catalyst.
* Downside is difficult to quantify.
* Upside/downside asymmetry is unattractive.

A research process that eliminates attractive-looking stocks is working properly.

Do not force a company to remain on the shortlist simply because it initially looked interesting.

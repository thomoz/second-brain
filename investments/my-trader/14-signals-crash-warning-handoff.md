## Assumptions tested — base-rate honesty

- **The framework doesn't time the top, only flags elevated risk.** The video says
  this explicitly: most of the 14 markers were already flashing in 1999, and the
  NASDAQ *doubled* over the next year before crashing. A "13/14 checked" reading is
  not a "sell this week" signal on its own terms — even the source doesn't claim that.
- **The margin-debt signal (#5) has a real asterisk the video glosses over.** It cites
  the same extreme YoY growth rate as having fired in 2000, 2007, *and 2021*. 2000 and
  2007 preceded severe, sustained crashes (-78% NASDAQ, -57% S&P respectively). 2021
  preceded the Jan 2022 top and a ~25% S&P bear market through Oct 2022 — real, but
  materially smaller and faster-recovering than the other two. If margin debt is meant
  to imply "2000/2007-scale event," 2021 is closer to a partial false positive on
  severity, even though direction (a top, then a decline) was right.
- **High-yield spreads specifically have a documented false-alarm history**: 2011
  (European sovereign-debt crisis), 2015-16 (energy/shale bust, spreads hit 887bps),
  and late 2018 all saw genuine spread widening with no recession following — each
  time met by a Fed pause/cut that calmed things back down. None of those episodes are
  reported as having closed *above 3.5% and held for a full month* (the video's
  specific trigger condition) — so the precise threshold hasn't obviously
  false-positived in what's checkable here, but this needs more digging before
  treating "3.5% for a month" as a clean, unambiguous sell signal.
- **Markers 1-4 and 11-13, as currently firing, are largely one story told seven
  ways, not seven independent confirmations** — in *this* cycle specifically, all
  seven happen to be downstream facets of the same fact: AI/hyperscaler
  infrastructure spending is increasingly debt-financed and outrunning cash
  generation. **Shaun's correction (2026-08-18), and the more important framing
  going forward**: this is a coincidence of *which sector is currently hot*, not a
  property of the checks themselves. Debt issuance, off-balance-sheet leases,
  vendor/circular financing, capex-vs-cash-flow, regulator warnings, CDS spreads,
  and funding-market stress are all **generic, sector-agnostic overheating checks**
  — they'd fire the same way for telecom in 2000, subprime/housing in 2007, or
  whatever the next hot sector is after AI. Built correctly (scanning for *which*
  sector is exhibiting these patterns, not hardcoded to AI/hyperscaler tickers),
  they stay independent and useful long after this specific AI cycle resolves.
  Treating today's overlap as evidence the checks are redundant would be the wrong
  lesson — the overlap is real *this cycle* but not structural to the checks.
- **The euphoria cluster (5-9: margin debt, IPOs, retail leverage, insider selling,
  Super Bowl ads) is a genuinely separate, more independent set** — these track
  investor/market *behavior* rather than the AI-capex-financing story specifically,
  so they add real incremental signal on top of markers 1-4/11-13 rather than
  restating them.

**Original read**: only 3 signals looked like a clean automated build, with ~8 more
treated as inherently periodic/manual. **Shaun's decision (2026-08-18): push past
that — automate as many of the 14 as possible, run daily regardless of each
signal's underlying update cadence** (a daily job can just re-check a quarterly
filing every day and only report a change when one actually appears — that's what
catches an off-cycle release the same day it lands, rather than waiting for a fixed
review date). Revised per-marker feasibility with that framing:

- **Straightforward daily-checkable now**: #14 high-yield spread (FRED), #5 margin
  debt (FINRA — updates monthly, but a daily job just re-reads "no change" most
  days), #8 insider selling (SEC EDGAR/OpenInsider, real-time-ish, `goat` scanner
  reusable), #10 most-valuable-company milestone (any daily market-cap data source,
  trivial to check daily even though it rarely changes).
- **Daily-checkable against whichever issuer(s) the sector-detection layer
  currently flags, updates only when new filings land**: #4 capex-vs-cash-flow,
  #2 off-balance-sheet leases (both from yfinance/EDGAR quarterly financials — a
  daily job just polls for whether a new 10-Q/10-K has posted since the last
  check), #12 credit spread in the hot sector (real CDS pricing is paywalled —
  proxy via issuer bond-yield spread vs. Treasury, checkable daily from free
  bond-yield data), #9 Super Bowl ad share (fires once/year but costs nothing to
  daily-check for "did a new one just air"). None of these are hardcoded to
  today's AI names — see the sector-detection decision below.
- **Needs a news/filing-scan approach rather than a clean numeric API, but still
  automatable as a daily job**: #1 debt issuance, #3 vendor/circular financing,
  #6 IPO/equity issuance, #7 retail/leveraged ETF flows, #11 regulator warnings,
  #13 funding-market stress — no single free structured feed found for any of
  these, but a daily news/RSS/EDGAR-8-K scan against known trigger phrases and a
  hot-sector/company watchlist is a real design, not a dead end. Build quality
  depends on finding decent free sources at build time; flag as the highest-effort
  bucket, not an impossible one.

None of the 14 are being written off as manual-only going in — the build will
surface which ones end up needing a manual/approximate fallback (e.g. "unknown"
the way `macro_indicators.py` already gracefully degrades when `FRED_API_KEY` isn't
set), rather than deciding that now.

## Decisions so far (2026-08-18)

- **Standalone** — not folded into my-trader's existing per-holding Monitor. Its
  own report, own schedule.
- **No scoping to a recommended action.** The tool's job is to report where each
  of the 14 signals currently stands and alert when one newly fires — not to
  recommend trimming any specific bucket/holding. Shaun makes that call himself
  once he sees the state.
- **Automate as many of the 14 as possible**, not just the easy 3 — see revised
  feasibility notes above.
- **Runs daily**, specifically *because* the underlying data doesn't all update on
  the same cadence — a daily check is what catches a quarterly filing or an
  ad hoc regulator report the same day (or the next day) it lands, rather than
  waiting for a fixed monthly/quarterly review date and missing the actual release
  timing.
- **The 7 markers (1-4, 11-13) are not one redundant story** — they're generic,
  sector-agnostic overheating checks that happen to all be pointing at AI/
  hyperscaler names *this cycle*. Build them to scan for *which* sector/company is
  exhibiting the pattern, not hardcoded to today's AI tickers, so they stay
  independently useful once this cycle resolves and something else gets hot next.

## Decisions, round 2 (2026-08-18)

- **Sector-detection logic built from day one, and it's the single shared input
  every per-issuer marker draws from — not just markers 1-4/11-13.** On Shaun's
  direct pushback (2026-08-18): a marker hardcoded to "Oracle" or "Nvidia,
  Palantir, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle, Microsoft" only works for this AI cycle and
  goes stale/useless the moment a different sector overheats next — which defeats
  the entire premise of the tool (find the *next* crash, not just re-confirm this
  one). The corrected architecture: one sector-detection layer produces a
  **dynamic "currently hot" company/sector watchlist**, refreshed on an ongoing
  basis (not just set once at build time), and every per-issuer marker —
  1 (debt issuance), 2 (off-balance-sheet leases), 3 (vendor financing),
  4 (capex-vs-cash-flow), 8 (insider selling), 11 (regulator warnings),
  12 (credit spread), 13 (funding-market stress) — reads from that same watchlist
  rather than each hardcoding its own company list. Today that watchlist resolves
  to Oracle/Nvidia/Microsoft/Meta/Amazon/Alphabet/Coreweave/Palantir because AI is
  what's hot; the checks themselves must not know or care that it's AI. This is
  the biggest single scope item in the eventual plan — flag it as such when this
  goes to `/plan-feature`.
- **New sibling project**: `investments/14-crash-signals-daily-check/` (working
  name from Shaun), alongside `my-trader/`, `briefs-finance/`, and `goat/` — not
  folded into my-trader. Own `pyproject.toml`, own report file(s), own scheduled
  job, per the existing uv-workspace pattern. Reads my-trader's/goat's shared DB
  and reuses `goat`'s OpenInsider plumbing across the package boundary the same way
  `goat` already imports from `mytrader`.
- **Alerting**: daily WhatsApp push — a short summary plus an explicit warning
  clause when any signal is newly firing (not a dump of all 14 every day). The
  full 14-signal report is written every run regardless of whether anything changed.
- **Deployment**: the VPS runs the daily job (same pattern as Goat's insider-scan
  timer and my-trader's Monitor timer) — it has to, since the WhatsApp push and the
  scheduled cadence both depend on the VPS's always-on systemd timers, not a
  Windows machine that isn't always on. The report file syncs back to the local
  vault through the existing git vault-sync mechanism (same mechanism that already
  brings `monitor-report.md`/`insider-scan-report.md` back from the VPS today) —
  no new sync mechanism needed, just make sure this tool's report path sits inside
  the synced vault tree like the others.
- **Marker 8 (insider selling) reuse assessed**: `goat/goat/openinsider.py`'s
  `fetch_screener_filings(tickers_list, trade_type, min_value)` is directly
  reusable as-is — it's a pure data-fetch function (OpenInsider screener scrape),
  not coupled to goat's holdings-watch orchestration. Marker 8 needs a different
  shape of check than what `goat/insider_scan.py` currently does, though:
  `run_holdings_watch()` is a per-filing, pct-of-position alert against goat's own
  held tickers; marker 8 wants a **12-month aggregate net-selling trend** (total
  sold vs. total bought) against the sector-detection layer's current hot-company
  watchlist (today that resolves to Nvidia, Palantir, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle,
  Microsoft — the names the video and fact-check both named — but it's the
  watchlist's output, not a constant baked into this check; corrected 2026-08-18,
  see the sector-detection decision above). Plan: import and reuse
  `openinsider.fetch_screener_filings()` directly, write a new aggregation check
  on top (sum sale value vs. purchase value over a trailing 365-day window per
  ticker, iterating whatever the watchlist currently contains) rather than reusing
  `insider_scan.py`'s orchestration logic, which doesn't fit this shape.
- **CDS subscription cost, researched**: there is no affordable single-name
  corporate CDS data source. Real institutional providers (Markit/S&P Global CDS
  Pricing Data, CMA, SOLVE, Bloomberg's CDSW function) don't publish retail
  pricing — access requires an institutional account, and Bloomberg Terminal
  itself (which does have this) runs **~$31,980/year, single seat, 2-year minimum
  commitment**, with no discounted individual/retail tier. EODHD's affordable
  APIs ($19.99-$99.99/mo) only cover *sovereign* CDS/credit-risk data, not
  single-name corporate CDS like Oracle's — doesn't solve marker 12. **Verdict:
  paying for real CDS data isn't viable at personal-tool budget; build marker 12
  on the bond-yield-spread-vs-Treasury proxy for the same issuer instead**,
  clearly labeled as a proxy (not true CDS pricing) in the report.
- **Marker 12's proxy must stay issuer-specific — it is NOT the same series as
  marker 14, on Shaun's direct question (2026-08-18).** Marker 14 (FRED
  `BAMLH0A0HYM2`) is a broad-market aggregate blended across hundreds of junk
  issuers — a general risk-appetite gauge. Marker 12's whole thesis is
  *divergence*: one hot-sector name's credit deteriorating while the broad market
  (marker 14) stays calm — exactly what happened with Oracle's CDS (145→215bps)
  against a flat 2.7% aggregate spread. Reusing marker 14's series for marker 12
  would collapse it into a duplicate, lagging copy of marker 14 and destroy the
  early single-name-divergence signal that's the entire point of the marker.
  **Concrete free source found**: FINRA runs its own free TRACE API
  (`developer.finra.org`) with individual-CUSIP transaction data (price, yield,
  volume) for specific corporate bonds — pick a specific bond CUSIP for whichever
  issuer the sector-detection layer currently flags as hot (Oracle today, per its
  July 2026 downgrade/CDS-spike story — but a dynamic pick, not a permanent target;
  same correction as marker 8 above), read its yield, subtract a maturity-matched
  Treasury yield (also free, FRED/Treasury.gov constant-maturity series) to get an
  issuer-specific credit spread that keeps working when a different sector's
  bellwether name is the one under stress next cycle. This is a real credit-spread
  computation, not a hand-wavy substitute — it's priced off the cash-bond market
  rather than the CDS derivatives market (funding cost vs. default-insurance
  pricing, related but not identical instruments), which is a distinction worth
  stating plainly in the report rather than treating cash-bond spread and CDS
  spread as interchangeable.
- **What the proxy actually measures vs. true CDS, spelled out (2026-08-18)**: both
  are a read on "extra yield the market demands to hold this company's debt over
  risk-free" — same underlying question, two different markets. **CDS** is a
  derivatives contract; dealers/speculators price it purely on perceived default
  risk, without needing to own or trade the company's actual bonds — a cleaner,
  faster-reacting signal because it's a standardized contract independent of any
  one bond issue's quirks. **The bond-yield proxy** uses one of the company's real
  outstanding bonds (via TRACE), so its yield also carries that specific bond's own
  liquidity (how easily it trades), supply/demand, coupon, and callability
  characteristics — noise that has nothing to do with default risk. The two
  usually move together, but the gap between them (literally called the
  "CDS-bond basis" in finance) can widen during liquidity-stress periods — 2008
  was a notable case. **Net effect for this tool**: the proxy will still catch
  "this issuer's credit is deteriorating," just less cleanly/quickly than real
  CDS would — report it as a proxy, not as equivalent to the CDS number the video
  itself quotes.

## Lessons: What Performs Well During a Crash (vetted against Buffett/Dalio, 2026-07-11)

**Core beginner principle (Buffett)**: the highest-confidence long-term strategy is
low-cost broad index funds, bought consistently, held indefinitely, backed by a cash
buffer. Everything below is a *satellite*, not a replacement for that.

**Gold — hold small, permanently, as ballast (Dalio All Weather: ~5-10%)**
- Use physical gold or a gold ETF (e.g. GLD/PMGOLD), not gold mining stocks, for this
  role — gold itself is the stable diversifier; miners are a separate, much more
  volatile, higher-risk bet on operating leverage.
- Gold miners (GDX) are a tactical, higher-volatility trade for money you can actively
  manage and are willing to trim after a crisis resolves — not a buy-and-forget holding.
  Historical drawdowns of -80% are real and have happened (2011-2015).

**Long bonds — situational, not universal**
- Long-duration Treasuries (TLT) help specifically in deflationary/rate-cutting
  recessions (2008: +14%) and hurt badly in inflationary/rate-hiking ones (2022: -30%+).
  Don't treat as an automatic crash hedge — it depends on *why* the market is falling.

**Farmland REITs — not the same asset as owning land**
- LAND/FPI are publicly traded small-cap stocks, correlated with interest rates and
  broader equity sentiment — both fell hard in 2022-23 rate hikes. FPI also had a 2018
  governance scandal. True farmland diversification benefits (Bill Gates, pension
  funds) come from *direct* land ownership, which these REITs don't replicate. Treat as
  a small, optional equity-income position, not a crisis hedge.

**Managed futures (DBMF/KMLM) — skip for now**
- Legitimate institutional concept ("crisis alpha"), but retail ETFs are new
  (2019-2022 launch), higher-fee, and unproven long-term. Not something Buffett or
  mainstream beginner advisors recommend as a foundational holding — revisit once the
  core portfolio and gold sleeve are established.

**When to buy/hold/sell, as a beginner**
- Index funds + cash buffer: never timed, always accumulating.
- Gold (ETF/physical): buy gradually, hold permanently as a small % — don't try to
  time entry/exit.
- Gold miners, long bonds, farmland REITs: these are *tactical* — sized small,
  bought when cheap/before you expect volatility, and can be trimmed once a crisis
  has clearly passed and prices have recovered. Not permanent core holdings.
- Source: "4 Assets That Doubled During Every Depression" (YouTube, transcript in
  `investments/my-trader/transcripts/`) — historical figures are largely accurate but
  the video is promotional in tone; treat its specific stock picks and 10-20%
  allocation framework as one opinion, not settled fact.

## Lessons: Protecting Purchasing Power — Inflation / Dollar Debasement (vetted against Buffett/Dalio, 2026-07-11)

Source: "Your Money Is About To Be Worth A Lot Less" (YouTube, transcript in
`investments/my-trader/transcripts/`). Unusually well-grounded for this genre — its
three headline claims were independently fact-checked and confirmed: US CPI 4.2%
(May 2026, accelerating on an energy shock), US Dollar Index down ~9.4-9.6% in 2025
(worst year since 2017), gold up 60-67% in 2025 (one of its strongest years since
1971). Still wrapped in "free video, get rich in this crash" promotional framing —
treat the packaging skeptically even though the numbers check out. The core thesis
(structural deficits → financial repression → currency debasement → gold as hedge)
is essentially Ray Dalio's own published "Big Debt Cycle" framework, not fringe.

**Idle cash is a real, quiet cost right now** — new lesson, distinct from the
crash-timing lessons above. With savings-account rates around 1-2% and inflation at
4.2%, cash sitting idle has a *negative real return* (losing purchasing power even
as the number goes up). For the portion of cash that's genuinely for liquidity (not
the crash-deployment buffer), consider I-bonds (US Treasury, rate adjusts with
inflation, but capped at $10k/year and locked for 1 year) or short-term Treasuries
over a plain savings account. Doesn't apply to the emergency/crash-deployment
buffer itself — that's deliberately liquid cash by design, not a yield-seeking
position.

**Gold's actual job — reinforces Bucket 3 framing, doesn't change it**
- Gold is a hedge, not a growth investment — "not supposed to make you rich, supposed
  to keep you from getting poor." Confirms the existing permanent-ballast framing
  above rather than adding anything new.
- Real caveat, accurate: gold went essentially nowhere from 1980-2000 while stocks
  boomed — a 20-year stretch of underperformance. Gold being strong right now
  (structural deficits, weak dollar, central bank buying) doesn't mean it's always
  the right call — regime-dependent, not a permanent free lunch.
- The video's "gold has beaten inflation by 18x since 1971" stat is roughly correct
  order-of-magnitude (not fabricated), but it's a cherry-picked 55-year headline
  number — treat as directionally true, not something to extrapolate forward.

**Pricing-power stocks / stocks that increase with inflation — this is Buffett's
actual answer to inflation, and it's literally Shaun's own existing criteria**
- Buffett doesn't hedge inflation with gold (he avoids it) — his real answer is
  owning businesses that can raise prices without losing customers (his own
  See's Candies example). This is an exact match for the "Companies must have the
  following" criteria already in this file — worth treating that criteria as
  Shaun's actual inflation hedge for the equity portion of the portfolio, not just
  a generic quality filter.
- Dividend aristocrats (25+ consecutive years of dividend increases) are a related,
  more mechanical version of the same idea — a company still growing its dividend
  above the inflation rate is growing your real income, not just nominal income.

**Real estate / REITs — new asset class, not yet discussed or added to any bucket**
- General real estate (not just farmland) has real inflation-hedging mechanics:
  rents rise with inflation, fixed-rate mortgages get cheaper in real terms over
  time. US home prices rose ~55% 2020-2025 vs. ~25% CPI over the same period.
  REITs (legally required to distribute 90%+ of taxable income) give exposure
  without buying property directly. Not yet vetted against the same rigor as
  farmland REITs above (governance risk, rate sensitivity likely apply similarly)
  — flag as a candidate for Bucket 1 or a new bucket, not yet assessed.

**Geographic/currency diversification — reinforces the domicile question already
open in `tool-preplan.md`**
- Dollar weakness is specifically a *dollar* problem — international equities
  denominated in other currencies can gain for a USD-based investor purely from
  currency movement, before the investment's own return even counts. 2025 was a
  real example: foreign stocks outperformed US stocks partly for this reason.
  Reinforces (doesn't resolve) the open VOO/VTI vs. VGS/VAS domicile question —
  as an Australian investor, some of this diversification may already come
  naturally through AUD-denominated holdings rather than needing separate action.

## Lessons: Reading Late-Cycle / Recession Warning Signals (vetted, 2026-07-11)

Source: "HOLY SH*T! The Banks Are About to INTENTIONALLY CRASH the Economy!"
(YouTube, transcript in `investments/my-trader/transcripts/`). **Bigger conflict of
interest than the previous two sources** — this one pitches paid trading-signal
subscriptions (CTA Timer Pro / Momentum Timer Pro) mid-video, giving the creator a
direct financial incentive to make the narrative feel urgent. Treat accordingly.

**The framing is not credible — the data underneath it is.** Three specific claims
were independently fact-checked and confirmed accurate: US consumer credit
unexpectedly contracted in May 2026 (first drop since Nov 2024, vs. an expected
$17.5B gain), average credit card interest rates ~22.15% and not falling even as
the Fed cuts, and existing home sales fell 2.4% month-over-month in June 2026.

**Reject the conspiracy framing.** The video claims banks are *intentionally*
orchestrating a downturn to profit and manipulate the Fed into cutting rates as a
"scapegoat." That's not how this works. When the yield curve inverts, banks borrow
short and lend long, so lending becomes structurally unprofitable — every bank
independently tightens credit in response to the *same* incentive. That looks
coordinated from the outside but is correlated rational behavior, not a plot. The
Fed cutting rates in response to weakening data is its literal stated job (dual
mandate: employment + price stability), not bank pressure. This is a real,
well-documented **late-cycle credit-cycle pattern** — the same territory Dalio's
own debt-cycle work covers — not an intentional conspiracy.

**What's actually useful here: a real leading-indicator checklist.** Strip the
narrative out and this is a legitimate list of things worth monitoring for
crash/recession timing (relevant to Bucket 2's "buy before a crash" trigger, and to
the tool's "Monitor" requirement in `tool-preplan.md`):
- **Yield curve (10yr minus 2yr Treasury)** — inversion has preceded most modern
  recessions; watch for it rolling over/re-normalizing, not just the inversion itself
- **Bank lending standards (Fed Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, SLOOS)** — net
  % of banks tightening commercial/industrial lending standards; rising = credit
  conditions genuinely tightening, a real economic drag, not narrative
- **Consumer credit growth (Fed G.19 release)** — unexpected contraction (like May
  2026) signals consumers/lenders both pulling back
- **Credit card delinquency rate vs. real income growth** — delinquencies rising
  while income growth slows has preceded every recession since the mid-90s in this
  data
- **Existing home sales + first-time buyer share** — sensitive to affordability/
  rate conditions, an early-ish signal
- **Retail sales vs. income growth** — sales holding up while income growth stalls
  is unsustainable (consumers running down savings/credit), a warning sign

None of these justify market-timing trades on their own, but as a set they're a
reasonable, legitimate answer to the open "what does Monitor actually check?"
question — worth carrying into the tool design, stripped of this source's
promotional framing.

**Additional indicators added 2026-07-16** (institutional/regulatory sourced, not
video transcripts — no promotional conflict-of-interest to flag on these). Split
into the same three jobs as above: valuation, recession-onset, and credit stress.

- **Buffett Indicator (total market cap / GDP)** — valuation gauge, not a timing
  trigger by itself. Buffett's own stated range: 75-90% = fair, >120% = overvalued.
  Can stay elevated for years (it did through most of the 2010s), so "expensive"
  is not the same as "about to fall" — pair with a recession-onset signal below
  before treating it as actionable.
- **Shiller CAPE ratio** — earnings-based valuation gauge, complements the Buffett
  Indicator's GDP-based approach. Same caveat applies: stretched valuation alone
  isn't a timing trigger.
- **Sahm Rule** (3-month average unemployment rate vs. its 12-month low; +0.5pp =
  recession signal) — the strongest pure recession-timing indicator on this list.
  Flagged every US recession since 1970 with no false positives, and it's
  real-time (monthly jobs data), unlike GDP which confirms a recession after the
  fact.
- **High-yield credit spread (OAS)** — the bond market's own pricing of default
  risk. Useful as a counter-check against the valuation metrics above: spreads can
  stay tight even when equity valuations look extreme, meaning credit markets
  aren't (yet) pricing in the same stress the valuation gauges imply.
- **Aggregate S&P 500 earnings growth / analyst estimate revisions** — watch for
  the *direction* flipping (analysts cutting estimates after a run of upgrades),
  not the absolute growth number. That flip is historically the real warning
  sign, not earnings softness on its own.
- **Private credit market stress** — direct lending default rates, BDC
  (business development company) unrealised losses as a % of NAV, and PIK
  (payment-in-kind) income share of BDC portfolios. This is a newer, more opaque,
  fast-growing credit channel (~$1.8-2T market) sitting partly outside the
  traditional bank system that SLOOS/consumer-credit data already covers — the
  Fed, IMF, and FSB have all flagged it as a vulnerability worth watching, though
  the consensus as of mid-2026 is that it isn't yet a systemic risk. PIK income
  rising is a particularly useful early tell: it means borrowers are paying
  interest with more debt instead of cash because they can't service it normally.
- **Geopolitical risk** — a different shape of signal from the rest of this list:
  event-driven and qualitative rather than a smooth economic series, but it has a
  quantifiable proxy (the Fed-maintained Caldara-Iacoviello **Geopolitical Risk
  (GPR) Index**, tallies newspaper coverage of geopolitical tensions monthly) and
  transmits into markets through specific, trackable channels: oil/energy prices,
  shipping chokepoints and freight/insurance costs, trade-route fragmentation, and
  sanctions. Not a "check a number" indicator like the others — more a "which
  active flashpoints could hit a channel we're exposed to" watch. Live example as
  of mid-2026: the Middle East conflict has effectively closed parts of the Strait
  of Hormuz (~20M barrels/day and 30% of seaborne hydrocarbons transit it),
  sending WTI crude from ~$60 to ~$100/barrel and making energy the top-performing
  S&P sector YTD (~+40%) — a concrete example of geopolitical risk showing up as a
  sector-level price shock rather than a broad market crash signal. Russia-Ukraine
  remains a second live, ongoing flashpoint. Relevant to sector concentration
  (e.g. HDV's ~20% energy weighting flagged in `tool-preplan.md`) as much as to
  crash-timing generally.

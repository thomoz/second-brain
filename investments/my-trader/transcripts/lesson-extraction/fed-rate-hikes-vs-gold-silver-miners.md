Can the Fed actually crash gold and silver prices just by raising the Fed funds rate, which is effectively the short-term interest rate? Stackers seem to get a little antsy anytime the Fed talks about raising interest rates. But what does the data show? Not just the repeated narrative. In this episode, we're going to look at 50 plus years of rate hiking data to get to the bottom of this as to whether gold, silver, and even the mining stocks. How are they all impacted? If the Fed does indeed hike interest rates.

All right, episode two of Danny Cosm. Episode one is in the books. Higher rates are automatically bad for gold. How many times have we heard that so far? How many times have we actually seen that play out? We just saw it play out this past Friday. The Fed at Jackson Hole Fed Chairman Walsh, just the mere hinting of a 25 basis point rate hike sent gold and silver coming down over 3% in this case for both metals. But what does the data actually say?

First data point: 28% correlation between the nominal Fed funds rate and gold since 1971 (when we went off the gold standard). Statistically weak and insignificant — about 28% of the variability between the two is explained by the relationship, 72% is noise. From a 50+ year correlation study from bulliontradingLLC.com. Visualization: Fed funds rate in black, gold in gold, no tight correlation on the surface.

Another statistic from the World Gold Council: they studied 44 hikes and found that over 50% of Fed hike days from 1997 to 2023 saw gold close positive, not negative. Counterintuitive — the opposite of the narrative. A 26-year study.

So two statistics either pointing that there's no relationship or that the relationship is actually the polar opposite.

Last six great rate hiking cycles:
- 1983 to 1984: gold down 29%
- 1988 to 1989: gold down 12%
- 1994 to 1995: gold pretty much flat
- 1999 to 2000: gold up 4%
- 2015 to 2018: gold up 24%
- 2004 to 2007: gold up 88%
- Plus the current cycle.

Gold by itself: this rate hiking cycle began February 2022. Gold was at ~$1,900. During that time frame gold has gone up as much as 200% (a tripling), and now we're currently at about 134% (over a doubling), given the Fed funds rate now around 5.3%. So a five percentage point move in the Fed funds rate has brought a doubling-to-tripling.

Hone in on 83-84 and 88-89 — both saw gold go down as the Fed hiked. In 83-84 gold went down 29% (from 491 to 348 an ounce, about $150), during a time of Paul Volcker where real rates stayed positive. In 88-89 a second tightening leg dropped gold further even with inflation already broken from its 1970s high. Real Fed funds rate chart: in 83-84 real rates stayed positive; in 88-89 real rates went from 2.3 to 4.2%. And that sent gold lower. The key point: real rates.

Opposite end: 2004 to 2007. Fed funds rate went from 1% to 5.25%. Gold skyrocketed up 88%, from $396 to $744. What made this different from the 1980s? Even though the Fed hiked more on a percentage basis, real rates stayed the same — real rates went from 2.15% to 2.26%, basically noise. The thing that matters is real rates, not nominal rates. When the Fed talks about raising 25 basis points (0.25%), ask whether that's really keeping track with inflation — if not, real rates are still far too low, meaning gold, silver, and mining stocks will probably be unaffected.

In that same 2004-2007 period, energy prices exploded — Brent crude went from 26.50 to $71 a barrel, a 270% move. Central banks were selling not buying gold — 1,550 tons of official gold sales acted as a $45/oz drag, but gold rallied anyway (central banks sell into strength). Today central banks are buying gold as the price rises — they don't see this as a top, unlike 2004-2007. And real rates stayed flat.

2015 to 2018: gold bottomed literally the day after the first hike, at around 1047-1050. The 2016 average was around 1250 — about a 25% move after the initial rate hike. Gold moved in tandem with the Fed funds rate (with a lag), as if the Fed funds rate was sending gold upward. 2017 gold went nowhere sideways ~1257; 2018 closed the year around 1300. Total of nine hikes before the cycle ended. 24% total return on gold for the cycle even as the Fed hiked nine times from 0.25 to 2.5%. Looks like the market had already priced in rate hikes — classic sell the rumor, buy the news, shown up across four decades of cycles: gold tends to underperform the runup to liftoff, then once rate hikes actually occur gold outperforms stocks and the dollar in the next 6 to 12 months.

2022: the fastest rate hiking cycle in 40 years. Fed funds rate went from 0% to between 5.25 and 5.5%. Never in history have we seen the Fed funds rate climb that fast since the early 1980s. Gold didn't collapse through the cycle. Initial hike — gold moved higher on the news, then the Ukraine war started; gold went from a high of ~2,000 to ~1,600 (a 20% decline), but once rates stabilized gold went from 1600 back up to ~2,000 by 2024, back to where it was at the start of the cycle. Unaffected by the 0% to 5.25% move.

The real five-year yield went from minus 1.8% to 2%. If the old gold-to-real-yield relationship had held, gold should have fallen about 58% to $830. But it fell only 17% and bottomed around 1640. Real yields then eased to 1.15 and gold rallied 22% back above 2,000 from that 1640 level. What picked up the slack: heavy central bank buying that isn't rate sensitive at all. That changed everything and has been the case since.

Silver: pretty much the same as gold except more amplified — "gold with more juice." Silver doesn't have an independent rate story like gold, but it tracks gold and is highly correlated, so it behaves the same way in rate hiking cycles with a larger swing in each direction. Gold is more of a monetary hedge (more central bank and ETF inflows); silver is a monetary hedge plus an industrial metal (solar panels, electronics, EVs pull on the same ounces), making it more volatile with more beta to gold's moves.

Mining stocks: don't appear to have any unique relationship to rates that gold and silver don't have. Effectively a leveraged bet on gold and silver. Costs are largely fixed; some miners need to finance their costs and as interest rates go up that eats into their costs. Example: mining stock A costs $1,500/oz to mine, gold at $1,800 = $300 profit margin. Gold from $1,800 to $2,100 is a 16.7% move, but profit margin goes from $300 to $600 — a 100% move, roughly 6x leverage. Not all miners are the same; no unique rate hiking story for the mining sector.

What the data (not the narrative) says:
1. Nominal rates are a weak signal — only 28% correlation since 1971. Look at real rates.
2. Real rates are the real driver. Watch inflation versus the hike pace, not the hike itself. Expect occasional breakdowns like 2022.
3. Markets front-run the first rate hike — gold has repeatedly bottomed right around liftoff, and it's not the last hike of the cycle.
4. Silver amplifies gold's direction — same signal, bigger swings both ways.
5. Miners are leveraged but not linear — about 2-3x multiple for most miners in the short term, but cost inflation and execution risk erode the edge over full cycles.

The sample size is genuinely small — only six rate hiking cycles since the 1970s. Despite the small sample it's still something — the only thing to point to. Look at all of it through the lens of probability.
